1. Introduction to X-Ray Radiation

Why Museum XRD/XRF Still Demands Respect

Our instruments analyze minerals, powders, and cultural heritage objects — not patients. That does not make them gentle. Enclosed analytical X-ray tubes can still deliver the annual occupational whole-body dose limit in a fraction of a second if someone enters the primary beam. “Research,” “low power,” and “cabinet” do not mean “safe to ignore.”

What are X-Rays?

X-rays are electromagnetic radiation with photon energies typically ranging from 100 eV to 100 keV (wavelengths ~0.01–10 nm). In laboratory XRD and XRF, characteristic X-rays are produced by electron bombardment of metal target anodes (commonly Cu, Mo, Cr, Ag, or Rh).

Production Mechanism

When high-energy electrons (accelerated through potentials of ~20–60 kV) strike a metal target, two types of X-radiation are produced:

1. Bremsstrahlung (Braking Radiation)
  • Continuous spectrum from electron deceleration
  • Energy range: 0 to the maximum electron kinetic energy
  • Intensity proportional to Z² and electron current
  • Contributes to background and scattered dose
2. Characteristic Radiation
  • Discrete lines specific to the target
  • Produced when inner-shell electrons are ejected and outer electrons fill the vacancy
  • Relevant to our lab:
    • Cu Kα: 8.04 keV (λ = 1.54 Å) — common for powder XRD
    • Mo Kα: 17.4 keV (λ = 0.71 Å) — common for single-crystal XRD
    • Rh target (~50 kV tube) — Horiba XGT-7200 micro-XRF

Properties Relevant to Safety

Penetrating Power

Attenuation: I(x) = I₀ × e(−μx). For Cu Kα (~8 keV): air μ ≈ 0.034 cm⁻¹; soft tissue ≈ 0.5 cm⁻¹; lead ≈ 130 cm⁻¹. Soft X-rays are strongly absorbed in tissue — high local dose near the beam.

Interactions with Matter
  • Photoelectric absorption (dominant at lab energies) — full energy transfer; main biological damage pathway
  • Compton scattering — partial energy transfer; creates scatter fields
  • Rayleigh (coherent) scattering — elastic; dominant in XRD diffraction geometry
Invisibility and Detection
  • Ion chamber survey meters (dose rate)
  • Geiger-Müller detectors (qualitative; energy-dependent)
  • Personnel dosimeters (TLD / OSL) — not required for normal use of these enclosed NHMLAC systems
Key Point: Laboratory sources often operate in the ~5–25 keV range (XRF tubes may go higher), where photoelectric absorption in tissue is strong. You cannot see, hear, or feel the beam — engineered controls and procedures are the only reliable protection.

2. Our Instruments

This training covers the three analytical X-ray systems at NHMLAC. Procedures below are facility safety procedures for this lab (based on instrument specs and ANSI N43.2 enclosed-system practice). Status lights, E-stops, and interlock behavior described here match the instruments as configured at NHMLAC.

Rigaku R-AXIS RAPID — Single-Crystal XRD

Full cabinet · Mo source · Imaging-plate / goniometer · Highest manipulation risk

  • Purpose: Single-crystal structure analysis (small / macromolecular crystals)
  • Source: Molybdenum (Mo) anode — treat the primary beam as extreme dose rate
  • Enclosure: Full radiation-shielded cabinet with access interlocks
  • Status lights: “X-rays on” and “Shutter open”
  • Emergency stop: Stop button on the left side of the instrument
  • Cryostream: Not installed on this system
  • High-risk operations: Crystal mounting, optical centering, work near the sample position with shutter potentially open, long data collections
  • Operator rule: Never place hands or tools in the beam path; confirm “X-rays on” / “Shutter open” are off before opening; cabinet closed before collection

Proto AXRD — Powder XRD

Enclosed · θ/θ powder diffractometer · Cu source · ANSI N43.2 compliant design

  • Purpose: Phase ID, crystallinity, lattice parameters on powders / bulk
  • Source: Copper (Cu) tube
  • Enclosure: Interlocked enclosure doors — opening the doors dumps the beam / disables X-rays
  • Status lights: “X-rays on” and “Shutter open”
  • Emergency stop: Stop button on the left side of the instrument
  • High-risk operations: Sample loading/unloading, cleaning spills, any urge to “just peek” with beam on
  • Operator rule: Load powder only with generator/shutter confirmed safe; never defeat the door interlock; spills do not justify open-door operation with X-rays on

Horiba XGT-7200 — Micro-XRF

Cabinet sample chamber · Rh tube (50 kV max) · Dual vacuum modes

  • Purpose: Non-destructive elemental analysis / mapping (Na–U range typical)
  • Source: Rhodium (Rh) tube, 50 kV max; mono-capillary microbeams
  • Enclosure: Fully shielded sample chamber — opening the chamber dumps the beam
  • Status indicator: Small red “X-rays” button/light on the top-front panel
  • Emergency stop: On the stage control box
  • Vacuum modes: Full vacuum (light-element sensitivity) vs partial/local vacuum (fragile / museum objects). Vacuum choice is sample care, not a radiation bypass. For partial-vacuum / heritage-object decisions, consult Aaron Celestian before running
  • High-risk operations: Opening or forcing the chamber during a run; assuming “XRF is weak”
  • Operator rule: Chamber closed and interlocked before X-rays; never force open during acquisition

Shared Dose Reality

For a typical 40 kV, 40 mA Cu tube at the exit port (~5 × 10⁵ R/hr): annual occupational limit (~5 rem) can be delivered in ~0.036 s; a potentially lethal dose (~500 rem) in ~3.6 s. Scatter at tens of cm can still be hazardous if shielding is compromised.

CRITICAL: There is no safe intentional primary-beam exposure. Enclosed systems are safe because of interlocks and procedures — not because the tube is harmless.

3. Biological Effects of X-Ray Radiation

Mechanisms of Radiation Damage

For laboratory X-ray energies (~5–25+ keV), energy deposition occurs through ionization (LET ≈ 2–3 keV/μm). Roughly ~30% of damage is direct (DNA ionization); ~70% is indirect via free radicals from water radiolysis (OH•, H•, e⁻aq).

DNA Damage Types
  • Single-strand breaks: usually repaired efficiently
  • Double-strand breaks: harder to repair; chromosomal aberrations possible
  • Base damage / cross-links: mutation risk if misrepaired

Deterministic Effects (Tissue Reactions)

Threshold effects — severity rises with dose. Hand/extremity exposure is the main concern for XRD/XRF operators.

  • Transient erythema: ~2 Gy
  • Main erythema: ~6 Gy (typically 7–14 days later)
  • Dry desquamation: ~10 Gy · Moist desquamation: ~15 Gy
  • Ulceration / necrosis: ~25–30+ Gy
Eye Lens
  • Detectable opacities: ~0.5 Gy acute
  • Vision-impairing cataract risk rises with higher doses; latency months to years

Stochastic Effects

No practical threshold; probability of cancer rises with dose. ICRP nominal risk coefficients are on the order of a few percent per Sv for fatal cancer. ALARA exists to keep risk as low as reasonably achievable.

ALARA

  • Dose limits: regulatory ceilings (e.g. 5 rem/year whole-body occupational)
  • Administrative goals: typically well below limits
  • Enclosed XRD/XRF goal: operator dose effectively zero (<10 mrem/year). Any measurable dose warrants investigation
Key Point: Skin injuries from primary-beam accidents can appear days later. Report any suspected exposure immediately — do not wait for symptoms.

4. Radiation Dose and Units

Units You Will See

  • Roentgen (R): exposure in air
  • Rad / Gray (Gy): absorbed dose (1 Gy = 100 rad)
  • Rem / Sievert (Sv): dose equivalent (for X-rays, QF/WR ≈ 1; 1 Sv = 100 rem)
  • Total dose = dose rate × time

Limits below follow common U.S. occupational frameworks (e.g. 10 CFR 835 / similar state tables). Your California registration and institutional policy govern day-to-day compliance — when in doubt, follow the RSO.

Extremity Emphasis for Our Lab

On the R-AXIS RAPID and when staging samples on the XGT-7200 or AXRD, hands are closest to the hazard. Extremity limits are higher than whole-body limits — that is not permission to accept hand exposure. A few seconds in a primary beam can still cause deterministic skin injury and burn a large fraction of the annual extremity limit.

Context

  • U.S. natural background ≈ 300 mrem/year
  • Chest X-ray ≈ 10 mrem
  • Proper enclosed XRD/XRF operator position: <0.1 mrem/hr; annual dose should be effectively zero
  • Primary beam at ~10 cm: 10⁴–10⁵ R/hr → annual limit in seconds or less
CRITICAL: Direct beam from a typical XRD tube can deliver the annual whole-body limit in well under a second. Interlocks and procedures exist because the physics is unforgiving.

5. Safety Features of X-Ray Equipment

ANSI N43.2 Essentials

  • Minimum of two independent interlocks for personnel safety
  • Fail-safe design (power loss → beam off / shutter closed)
  • Clear “X-RAY ON” / shutter status indication
  • Shielding such that external dose rates are negligible under normal use

Feature Map — Our Three Instruments

Shutters, Lights, and Labels

  • Shutters should fail closed; never assume — look at indicators
  • Red “X-RAY ON” (or equivalent) must be visible from access points
  • Trefoil + “CAUTION: X-RAY RADIATION…” labels must remain in place

Interlock Testing Cadence

  • Daily: visual check of lights and enclosure integrity (operator)
  • Quarterly: functional interlock test performed by Aaron Celestian — documented in the log
  • Annually: radiation survey / comprehensive safety inspection
CRITICAL: Never bypass, tape, wedge, or “temporarily defeat” an interlock. That converts a safe instrument into an immediate severe-injury hazard and is grounds for loss of authorization.

6. Safe Operating Procedures

Use this shared workflow on every instrument: Authorize → Inspect → Load → Close & verify indicators → Run → Shut shutter/generator → Unload → Log.

Universal Pre-Use Checklist

  • You are trained and authorized for this specific instrument (authorization only from Aaron Celestian or Kriss Leftwich)
  • Enclosure / chamber intact; no cracks, missing panels, or taped interlocks
  • Warning lights and status indicators appear functional
  • No “DO NOT OPERATE” tag present
  • Dosimetry is not required for normal enclosed-system use in this lab

Rigaku R-AXIS RAPID SOP (Safety)

  1. Authorize: Confirm SCXRD authorization is current.
  2. Inspect: Check full cabinet closed/intact; “X-rays on” and “Shutter open” indicators; know the left-side stop button location.
  3. Load: Open enclosure only with X-rays confirmed off and shutter closed. Mount and optically center the crystal without placing body parts in the beam path.
  4. Close & verify: Close the cabinet. Confirm interlocks satisfied and indicators match expected “ready” state.
  5. Run: Start collection from the control position. Do not open the cabinet during acquisition.
  6. Shut down: Close shutter; confirm “X-rays on” / “Shutter open” are off. Use the left-side stop button in an emergency.
  7. Unload & log: Open only after confirmed safe; retrieve sample; log usage and any anomalies.
RAPID focus: Mo source, full cabinet, no cryostream. Mounting and centering are the highest-risk moments. Never “feel for the beam.” Use cameras / optical alignment tools.

Proto AXRD SOP (Safety)

  1. Authorize: Powder XRD authorization current.
  2. Inspect: Doors close fully; “X-rays on” and “Shutter open” lights present and undamaged; know the left-side stop button.
  3. Load: With X-rays off / shutter closed, mount powder or bulk sample. Clean spills with beam off — never with the door open and tube energized.
  4. Close & verify: Close enclosure; confirm interlock; lights should not show X-rays on until you intentionally start.
  5. Run: Start scan. Opening the doors must dump the beam — if X-rays remain on with a door open, treat as emergency (Section 9).
  6. Shut down: End run; confirm shutter closed / X-rays off before opening. Use the left-side stop in an emergency.
  7. Unload & log: Remove sample; leave instrument in safe state; log run.

Horiba XGT-7200 SOP (Safety)

  1. Authorize: Micro-XRF authorization current. For partial-vacuum or fragile heritage objects, consult Aaron Celestian before the run.
  2. Inspect: Chamber seal/window intact; red “X-rays” indicator on the top-front panel; know the E-stop on the stage control box.
  3. Load: Place sample with X-rays off. Choose full vs partial vacuum for sample survival — radiation rules are identical in both modes.
  4. Close & verify: Close chamber; confirm interlock before enabling X-rays.
  5. Run: Acquire spectra/maps. Never force the chamber open mid-run. Opening the chamber must dump the beam.
  6. Shut down: Stop X-rays; vent per procedure; confirm the “X-rays” indicator is off. Use the stage-control-box E-stop in an emergency.
  7. Unload & log: Retrieve object carefully; log analysis and any chamber/seal concerns.
Best Practice: Always assume the beam is on until you have personally verified indicators and shutters. Software screens alone are not enough if lights disagree.

7. Time, Distance, and Shielding

TIME

Total Dose = Dose Rate × Time. Prepare tools and samples before opening an enclosure; automate where possible; do not linger near open beam paths.

RAPID: Plan crystal mounting so enclosure open-time is short and beam is confirmed off.

DISTANCE

Inverse square law: I(r) = I₀ × (r₀/r)². Doubling distance → intensity to 25%.

Distance from focus Relative intensity (point source approx.)
1×100%
2×25%
10×1%

AXRD / XGT: Work from the control position; do not lean into cabinets. Distance helps against scatter if shielding fails — it does not make primary-beam contact safe.

SHIELDING

I(x) = I₀ × e(−μx). For Cu Kα, fractions of a millimeter of lead can provide >99% attenuation. Enclosures and chambers are your shielding — do not modify them.

XGT-7200: The sample chamber wall/window is the shield. Compromised seals or forced openings defeat the design.

Key Point: On enclosed systems, Time/Distance/Shielding are backups. Interlocks and closed barriers are primary.

8. Hazard Recognition and Control

Danger Categories

Equipment Malfunctions
  • Warning light stays on with door/chamber open
  • Ability to energize X-rays with enclosure open
  • Damaged interlock switches, cracked shielding, missing panels

Immediate actions: Do not continue; kill power / E-stop; post DO NOT OPERATE; notify RSO.

Procedural Violations
  • Operating without training; defeating interlocks; unauthorized repairs
  • Alignment or mounting with body parts in the beam path

Instrument Scenarios

Scenario A — R-AXIS RAPID

While centering a crystal you keep a panel ajar “so you can reach in quickly,” and a colleague starts a short exposure from the software.

What went wrong: Enclosure not secured; reliance on habit instead of interlocks/indicators; hand potentially in scatter or beam path.

Correct action: Never start exposures with panels open. Close enclosure, verify indicators, then collect. If beam was on with access open — E-stop, report as suspected exposure pathway.

Scenario B — Proto AXRD

Powder spills on the stage mid-run. You open the door to wipe it while the scan continues so you “don’t lose the peak positions.”

What went wrong: Opened enclosure during operation; prioritized data over engineered controls.

Correct action: Abort/stop X-rays; confirm lights show off; then clean. Retake data if needed — skin is not worth a diffractogram.

Scenario C — Horiba XGT-7200

A fragile museum object is in partial vacuum. Mid-map you worry about vacuum and try to crack the chamber door “just a little” while X-rays are on.

What went wrong: Compromising the chamber barrier during generation; confusing sample-care mode with radiation safety.

Correct action: Stop X-rays first, then vent/open per procedure. Partial vacuum never allows open-chamber operation with beam on.

Classic Case Study (Any Enclosed System)

Hand enters primary beam for ~2 s after interlock defeat: doses of tens of Gy to the hand are plausible → erythema, blistering, medical follow-up. Lesson: never defeat interlocks.

Remember: You cannot sense X-rays. The difference between safe operation and injury can be milliseconds.

9. Emergency Procedures

Suspected Overexposure

  1. Stop X-rays immediately — shutter close, generator off, E-stop / main power as needed
  2. Move people away from the instrument
  3. Do not resume operation
  4. Notify supervisor and RSO (contacts below)
  5. Seek medical evaluation if advised
  6. Document: who, which instrument, time, tube settings if known, what was open, estimated duration, witnesses
  7. Preserve the scene (do not “fix” interlocks before investigation)

Interlock Failure / Beam-On with Access Open

  • Do not open further; use E-stop / kill power at the wall or generator
  • Evacuate immediate area; post DO NOT OPERATE
  • Call RSO; treat anyone who may have been near the opening as potentially exposed

Equipment Malfunction (Non-Radiation)

  • Water leak, unusual noise, vacuum failure, software crash: shut X-rays down safely, tag out if needed, notify steward/supervisor
  • Do not attempt HV / shielding repairs unless authorized

Fire / Building Emergency

  • If safe, shut generators off; evacuate per museum procedures; do not re-enter for samples

  • ANSI N43.2: Radiation safety for X-ray diffraction and fluorescence analysis equipment
  • California radiation machine regulations: Registration, surveys, and use of non-medical X-ray machines (follow CDPH / institutional radiation program requirements)
  • Institutional policy: NHMLAC training, authorization, and SOPs — these may be stricter than the minimum
  • Dose-limit science: Occupational limit tables (e.g. 5 rem whole-body) remain useful references even when DOE rules are not the facility’s legal driver
